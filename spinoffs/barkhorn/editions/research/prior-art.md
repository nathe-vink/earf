# Prior art: objects inside acrylic, and designed bubbles

Research for EARF's planned special editions: objects cast or laminated inside tinted cast acrylic, and editions with designed air bubbles. Public reading only. All sources accessed 2026-10-31.

**How to read the sourcing.** "(opened)" means the page was fetched and read. "(search result)" means the claim came from a search-engine summary of that page because the page itself returned 403 or would not open; treat those as likely but not checked. Anything marked **unconfirmed** could not be confirmed from any source.

## 1. Shiro Kuramata, Miss Blanche (1988)

**What it is.** A small armchair of clear cast acrylic with artificial red roses suspended in it, on four tubular aluminium legs. It is named after Blanche DuBois in Tennessee Williams's *A Streetcar Named Desire*. Kuramata was moved to make it after seeing Elia Kazan's 1951 film.
- Phillips editorial, https://phillips.com/article/96607712/miss-blanche-chair (opened; accessed 2026-10-31)
- 6sqft, https://www.6sqft.com/iconic-plastic-armchair-sells-at-sothebys-auction-for-409000/ (opened; accessed 2026-10-31)

**Who made it.** Ishimaru Co., Ltd., Tokyo, is named as manufacturer by Phillips, Sotheby's 2015 and Sotheby's 2024.
- https://phillips.com/detail/shiro-kuramata/NY050222/44 (opened; accessed 2026-10-31)
- https://sothebys.com/en/auctions/ecatalogue/2015/20th-century-design-l15670/lot.161.html (opened; accessed 2026-10-31)

Ishimaru also made the Feather stool, the Flower Vases and the Kyoto table (see section 2). The High Museum says Ishimaru was founded in 1969: https://high.org/collection/feather-stool/ (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).

**How it was made.**
- Kuramata first tried real roses. They burned in the acrylic resin, so he switched to artificial ones. His assistant Hisae Igarashi recalled him saying, "It has to be fake, because Blanche DuBois herself is a fake." (Phillips editorial, above; Sotheby's 2015 catalogue note, above.)
- The roses were positioned with tweezers in four moulds while the liquid acrylic cured (Phillips editorial, above).
- Each rose had to be held in place for long periods until the resin hardened (Sotheby's 2015 and 2024 catalogue notes).
- One account says the tweezers were withdrawn moments before the acrylic set. Another describes the acrylic being poured in several stages. Both came only from search results, not from an opened page, so these details are **unconfirmed beyond the Phillips "tweezers / four moulds" text**.
- Materials are given as "acrylic, artificial roses, anodised aluminium" (Sotheby's 2015), "acrylic resin, synthetic roses, anodized aluminum" (Phillips 2022), and "acrylic, artificial roses, aluminum pipe, stained alumite finish" (Friedman Benda, https://www.friedmanbenda.com/exhibitions/shiro-kuramata-design-as-poetry/ (opened; accessed 2026-10-31)).
- The Phillips editorial describes the legs as purple. So the legs are **dyed anodised (alumite) aluminium, not painted**. One early example sold at Christie's is catalogued as "epoxy-coated tubular aluminum": https://www.christies.com.cn/lot/lot-1808063 (opened; accessed 2026-10-31). It was given to Scott Crolla at Christmas 1989. The leg finish may therefore vary between examples.

**How many were made.** It was an edition of 56, "one for each year of Kuramata's life" (Phillips editorial; every auction page above). 6sqft says 56 had been produced by the time of his death in 1991. A search result says they were produced 1988–1998, which is **unconfirmed**.

**When it was shown.** It was first shown in 1988 at Tokyo Designers' Week, in the KAGU exhibition at the Axis Gallery Annex. It was then shown in Paris at Galerie Yves Gastou in 1989 (6sqft, above). Museum holdings include MoMA, SFMOMA, the Dallas Museum of Art and the Vitra Design Museum (6sqft). MoMA's own page (https://www.moma.org/collection/works/2630) returned 403.

## 2. Kuramata's other acrylic and inclusion pieces

- **Feather Stool (1990).** A block of clear acrylic with dyed feathers suspended in it, on aluminium. It was made by Ishimaru and first conceived for Spiral, the Tokyo gallery and shopping building. The High Museum says the piece contrasts weight with weightlessness and permanence with impermanence. Sources: https://high.org/collection/feather-stool/ (opened; accessed 2026-10-31). Brooklyn Museum gives "acrylic, aluminum, dyed mallard feathers, about 54 × 33 × 33 cm": https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/objects/224451 (search result; accessed 2026-10-31). Wright and Phillips list an example as no. 31 from an edition of 40 (search result, https://www.wright20.com/auctions/2023/03/design/268/print; accessed 2026-10-31).
- **Flower Vases / Ephemera (1989).** Made for Kuramata's 1989 solo show at Galerie Yves Gastou, Paris, and manufactured by Ishimaru. Each is a glass test tube for a single stem set inside an acrylic block, sometimes tinted pink or blue. Sources: Met, "Flower Vase #3", https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/491723; High, https://high.org/collection/flower-vase-2/; Brooklyn Ephemera floor vase (anodised aluminium, acrylic, glass, blue lacquer), https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/objects/225687 (all search results; accessed 2026-10-31).
- **Kyoto table and "Star Piece" terrazzo (1983).** Made for Memphis by Ishimaru. It is a terrazzo of concrete with coloured glass shards in place of stone chips, which Kuramata called "the debris of memory". It is not acrylic, but it is an inclusion in a cast matrix. Sources: Met, https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/747429; Centre Pompidou, https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/ressources/oeuvre/c88LX4 (search results; accessed 2026-10-31).
- **Laputa (1991).** A bed, Kuramata's last furniture design, made for a 1991 exhibition in Florence. Search results describe it as wrapped in Star Piece and satin. **No source confirms it is acrylic.** Treat it as not part of the acrylic lineage.
- **Acrylic Armchair (1988–89) and Glass Chair (1976).** MoMA pairs these as Kuramata's studies of transparency in glass and in acrylic: https://www.moma.org/collection/works/155077 (search result; accessed 2026-10-31).
- **Hiroshi Sugimoto.** Wright held a sale in January 2015 titled "Shiro Kuramata & Hiroshi Sugimoto: Works from the Absent Past", https://wright20.com/auctions/2015/01/shiro-kuramata-hiroshi-sugimoto-works-from-the-absent-past/109 (403; title from search result). What Sugimoto contributed, and any link to bubbles or optical glass, is **unconfirmed**.

## 3. Other prior art

### Gaetano Pesce, poured resin
- **Nobody's Perfect** was produced by Zerodisegno. The chairs are poured resin, each different in colour and form, and many are signed and dated on the back. The Cooper Hewitt holds one (gift of Zerodisegno, catalogued 2004), and it was shown in the 2003–04 National Design Triennial. Dates in dealer listings run from 2002 to 2010. Sources: https://si.edu/object/nobodys-perfect:chndm_2004-27-1 (403; search result); Phillips https://www.phillips.com/detail/加塔諾．佩斯/185860 (search result; accessed 2026-10-31).
- Pesce made a deliberate method of irregularity: no two pieces alike, and casting accidents left in. The Spaces (4 Sept 2026) covers his Broadway (epoxy) and Fish (polyurethane) series: https://thespaces.com/imperfect-perfection-the-rise-of-resin/ (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).

### Charles Hollis Jones, Lucite furniture
- Known as the "King of Lucite". He moved from Indiana to Los Angeles at 16. The **Wisteria chair (1968)** was made from intersecting acrylic sheets for Tennessee Williams. Other works include the Edison lamp (1970, bare bulb) and the Waterfall, Fleur de Lis and Metric lines. Clients included Sinatra, Lucille Ball and Johnny Carson, and he worked with John Lautner on more than a dozen projects. Wallpaper's obituary (21 Aug 2026) says he died aged 81: https://www.wallpaper.com/design-interiors/charles-hollis-jones-obituary (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).
- His work is fabricated sheet acrylic, with **no embedments**. It is relevant as the reference point for clear acrylic furniture glamour, not for inclusions.

### Lucite embedments, awards, deal toys and tombstones
- A "tombstone" was a plain, column-width newspaper notice of a financial deal. In the **1960s** banks began sealing these notices in clear Lucite slabs, which became the deal toy. Designs later turned playful (one example is a J.P. Morgan dinosaur head for a Universal deal), and production fell after 2008. Source: Financial History magazine, issue 116 (Winter 2016), https://fhmagazine.org/financial-history-issue-116-winter-2016/0015423001455208064/p10 (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).
- Wikipedia dates deal toys to the 1970s. It defines an embedment as paper, metal or acetate sealed in poured acrylic so it seems to float, and notes Lucite was DuPont's brand: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrylic_trophy (opened; accessed 2026-10-31). The 1960s versus 1970s start date is therefore **unresolved**.
- The Museum of London holds a Lucite tombstone (2008): https://londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/v/object-777935/lucite-tombstone (search result; accessed 2026-10-31).
- Vintage "rose-embedded Lucite" knobs and 1960s Italian Lucite "micro-cosmos" pieces with butterflies and flowers are common on resale sites. Roses in Lucite were a decorative cliché well before Miss Blanche. Sources: https://www.chairish.com/product/18272143/960s-rose-embedded-lucite-knobs-a-pair; https://www.1stdibs.com/furniture/decorative-objects/sculptures/figurative-sculptures/italian-lucite-micro-cosmos-butterfly-flowers-1960s/id-f_21923272 (search results; accessed 2026-10-31).

### DRIFT (Studio Drift): Fragile Future
- Real dandelion seed heads are picked by hand and glued seed by seed onto LEDs, mounted on three-dimensional phosphor-bronze circuits. The work is in the V&A's collection and is represented by Carpenters Workshop Gallery. Source: https://www.studiodrift.com/work/fragile-future (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).
- It began as Lonneke Gordijn's graduation project at Design Academy Eindhoven. It was shown at Salone del Mobile in **2007** and won the German Design Council's "Lights of the Future" prize in 2008 (search results, e.g. https://www.philamuseum.org/exhibitions/rhythms-of-nature-the-art-design-of-drift; accessed 2026-10-31). A **2005** start date is **unconfirmed**.
- Some editions add acrylic or Perspex: https://carpentersworkshopgallery.com/works/fragile-future-ff-3-17/ (search result).
- **"Ego" by DRIFT: unconfirmed.** No source found.

### Sabine Marcelis
- **Candy Cube (2014–ongoing).** A single-cast, highly polished resin cube, about 60 × 60 × 30 cm, with a soft colour gradient and a glowing edge, used as a side table or plinth. It is in MoMA's collection, and Marcelis also made resin display cubes for Céline in 2014. Sources: Side Gallery technical sheet, https://side-gallery.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/Sabine_Marcelis_2014_Technical_Sheet_Wall_Cube_Red.pdf (search result); MoMA https://www.moma.org/collection/works/479667 (search result); Icon, https://www.iconeye.com/design/sabine-marcelis (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).
- **Voie.** Neon tubes cast into coloured polyester resin, in limited editions (Icon, above). Side Gallery dates one Voie wall lamp to 2017: https://side-gallery.com/?p=1788 (search result).
- **Seeing Glass.** Layered polychrome glass, made with Brit van Nerven (Icon, above). The year is unconfirmed.

### Designed bubbles in glass (all generic techniques with long histories)
- **Graal (Orrefors).** Wikipedia shows a 1916 Graal vase designed by Simon Gate and blown by Knut Bergqvist: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orrefors_Glassworks (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).
- **Ariel (Orrefors, 1936–37).** Developed by Edvin Öhrström with Vicke Lindstrand and master blower Gustav Bergkvist. It builds on Graal: the pattern is sandblasted into a blank, which is then cased so that the cut channels become trapped air. It was first shown at the 1937 Paris Exposition. Sources: V&A https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O5552/ (opened); NGV https://ngv.vic.gov.au/explore/collection/work/20275 (search result: sandblasting); SNL https://snl.no/arielglass (search result: 1937 Paris). The name refers to the air (V&A).
- **Kosta.** Orrefors bought Kosta Boda in 1990, and Bergqvist had earlier worked at Kosta (Wikipedia, above). No Kosta-specific bubble technique was confirmed: **unconfirmed**.
- **Bullicante (Venetian controlled bubbles).** The blower dimples a hot gather, cases it with a second gather to trap the air, and blows it into an optic mould so the bubbles fall in a regular grid. Source: Blenko, https://blenko.com/blogs/tools-of-the-trade-1/tools-of-the-trade-tuesdays-bubble-optic-1 (opened; accessed 2026-10-31).
- **Steuben.** Frederick Carder used "bubbly" (air-trap) glass in the 1920s–30s. 1960s Steuben stems carry a single controlled bubble. Sources: https://glasscollection.cmog.org/objects/23910/vase (search result); 1stdibs listing for Steuben #7924 (search result).
- **Lalique and bubbles: unconfirmed.** No source found.
- **Subsurface laser engraving ("bubblegrams").** A focused pulsed laser makes micro-fractures at points inside clear glass, without touching the surface, and the points scatter light into a 3D image. Points placed too close merge into cracks. Source: FacFox, https://facfox.com/docs/kb/sub-surface-3d-laser-engraving-a-brief-explanation (search result; accessed 2026-10-31). Vendors call the usual K9 glass "borosilicate", but K9 is a crown glass. Whether this works well in cast PMMA is **unconfirmed** and would need a test.

### Resin artists and current gallery examples
- **Studio Nucleo (Piergiorgio Robino, Turin, founded 1997).** "Primitive" (2009) was the studio's first resin work. "Presenze" rebuilt furniture from 5 cm epoxy cubes. The Jade stools have oak logs submerged in resin. A Thonet chair cast in resin was shown with Ammann Gallery in 2016, and an antique ladder cast in clear resin at Salon Art + Design, New York, in 2015. Sources: https://www.6sqft.com/nucleos-jade-stools-crystallize-200-year-old-oak-branches/; https://www.dezeen.com/2016/06/20/nucleo-traps-thonet-chair-resin-ammann-gallery/amp/; https://www.dezeen.com/2015/11/12/nucleo-resin-metal-furniture-salon-art-design-ammann-gallery-new-york/ (search results; accessed 2026-10-31). Nucleo is the closest prior art for **a whole found object frozen in a clear block**.
- **Marcin Rusak, Flora.** Unsold flowers are cast in resin and the blocks cut to reveal the petals. "Flora Noir" opened in May 2017 at Twenty First Gallery, New York, and "Flora Contemporaria" (works dated 2021) was shown at the same gallery. Sources: https://www.dezeen.com/2017/05/16/marcin-rusak-adds-tropical-plants-flora-noir-furniture-collection-exhibition-twenty-first-gallery-new-york/; https://www.incollect.com/node/560300 (search results; accessed 2026-10-31). This is the strongest current "flowers in resin furniture" signature.
- **Paraforma (Valentina Grittini).** Resin pieces after Perriand's 1930s Table Studies, including the Lumen table, at Monologue, Mayfair, during London Design Festival 2026. The studio deliberately keeps the bubbles and impurities left by casting. Source: The Spaces, https://thespaces.com/imperfect-perfection-the-rise-of-resin/ (opened; accessed 2026-10-31). This is the most current **bubbles as feature** example.
- **Wonmin Park, Haze (Carpenters Workshop Gallery, Paris, 2015).** Pigmented resin used as a colour field. Shown again at TEFAF Maastricht 2023 and TEFAF New York 2025. Sources: https://www.dezeen.com/2015/08/31/wonmin-park-resin-table-bookcase-haze-furniture-series-carpenters-workshop-gallery-paris/amp/ (403; search result); https://carpentersworkshopgallery.com/fairs/tefaf-new-york-2025/ (search result).
- **Friedman Benda.** Showed Miss Blanche in its "dna10" anniversary show, and Joris Laarman's Bone Armchair (2008, marble resin). It also showed Maarten Baas's charred wood in epoxy in "An Accelerated Culture" (2019). Sources: https://www.1stdibs.com/introspective-magazine/friedman-benda-dna10; https://www.dezeen.com/2019/05/25/an-accelerated-culture-generation-x-friedman-benda-new-york/amp/ (search results). Its "Kuramata: Design as Poetry" show ran at the Vitra Design Museum Gallery from 19 Oct 2013 to 12 Jan 2014 (friedmanbenda.com, opened).
- **Philipp Aduatz, Sinking Chair.** 3D-printed concrete with red epoxy, Isola Design Gallery, Milan 2025: https://archup.net/philipp-aduatzs-sinking-chair-redefines-design-at-milan-show/ (search result).
- **Salon 94, R & Company, Patrick Parrish, Nina Johnson.** No acrylic or resin pieces with bubbles or inclusions were found for these galleries. Patrick Parrish (as Fisher Parrish) held a "Paperweight Show": https://www.sightunseen.com/?p=68202 (search result). R & Company lists The Haas Brothers, "Tree House", April–August 2026, which is not resin. These are **gaps, not negatives**: the galleries' own archives were not searched.
- **Dean Bradshaw** and **Fredrikson Stallard** resin with bubbles: **unconfirmed**. Nothing found. Fredrikson Stallard's known works (Species sofas 2015, Iris for Swarovski 2011) are not bubble resin: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fredrikson_Stallard (search result).

## 4. Generic and fair vs signatures to avoid

### Generic and fair (techniques and motifs nobody owns)
- **Embedment itself.** Placing an object in poured acrylic or resin has been a trade process since the 1960s (awards, paperweights, deal toys).
- **Staged pours and holding the object until it gels.** This is basic casting practice, not Kuramata's.
- **Laminations.** Bonding cast or cut layers with a print, film, mesh, veneer or coloured sheet between them.
- **Bubbles.** Trapped, controlled or sandblast-and-case bubbles go back to Graal (1916), Ariel (1937), bullicante and Carder's air-trap glass. Casting bubbles kept on purpose is now a common studio idea (Paraforma, Pesce's irregularity).
- **Subsurface laser point clouds** are an industrial process.
- **Specimens in general**: botanical, mineral, mechanical, a component of the product itself (a driver, a filament, a circuit).
- **Tinted cast acrylic as colour and light.** This is EARF's own baseline and a long-shared vocabulary (Kuramata's tinted vases, Wonmin Park, Hollis Jones).

### Signatures to avoid (a specific motif plus form that one name owns)
- **Red or pink roses floating in a clear seat on slim tubular legs.** Miss Blanche. The same goes for any scattered-flower field in a clear chair or stool block.
- **Feathers suspended in a clear stool block** (Kuramata Feather stool).
- **A single test-tube stem vase sunk in a tinted acrylic block** (Kuramata Flower Vase).
- **Coloured glass shards in a terrazzo matrix**, called "Star Piece" (Kuramata).
- **Fresh flowers cast in resin and cut open as furniture or cabinet panels** (Rusak, Flora).
- **A whole found piece of furniture or a log frozen in a clear block** (Nucleo).
- **Dandelion seed heads on LEDs or bronze circuits** (DRIFT, Fragile Future).
- **Soft single-colour gradient in a polished resin cube or plinth with a glowing edge**, and **neon tubes cast in coloured resin** (Marcelis, Candy Cube and Voie).
- **Announcement or business object in a Lucite slab, presented as a commemorative "tombstone"**. Too close to the deal-toy genre to read as anything else.
- **Sandblasted figurative pattern sealed as air channels in thick cased glass** (Orrefors Ariel). This is a historic trademark-like look, though the underlying air-trap principle is free.

### The rule
**Use any technique; never pair someone's signature subject with their signature form. The thing inside must come from EARF's own world (sound, light, the product's own parts) and must be arranged to an EARF logic, so that it could not be mistaken for a Kuramata, Rusak, DRIFT, Nucleo or Marcelis piece at a glance.**
