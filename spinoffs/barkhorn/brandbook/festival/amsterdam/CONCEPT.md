## The work

A globe two metres across, drawn in light.

It is made of six discs of cast acrylic, each tinted all the way through in one colour:
- **Three meridians**, in green, orange and violet, stand on edge and cross at the poles.
- **Three parallels**, in red, blue and yellow, ring them at the equator and at forty degrees north and south.

The discs are slotted into one another and bonded clear where they cross, so the globe is open: you see through it,
and through one colour into the next. Its axis leans 23.4 degrees, as the Earth's does.

By day it is a piece of coloured glass the size of a small room. The sun goes through each disc and throws its colour
on the ground, and where two discs cross the colours mix.

At night, light is fed into the discs from inside the base and travels through the acrylic to their polished edges. A
disc of cast acrylic carries light the way a fibre does, so the faces stay dark and clear and **the rims glow**. Six
lines of colour, each the circle of its disc, draw the globe in the air.

There's no screen, no projection and no moving part. The light is steady, and the work is the material doing what it
does.

## The idea

[THEME: Edition 16's theme, from the artist briefing. One paragraph that reads the work through it, written once the
theme is known. Hold to what the work is: a model of the planet you can stand inside the light of, made from one
material in six colours.]

A globe is the oldest way of holding the whole world in front of you. Most globes are solid: a surface of countries
with nothing inside. This one is only lines, the meridians and the parallels, the grid we drew on the planet to find
our way around it. With the surface gone, the light is what's left, and you can look straight through the world to the
city on the other side.

Each line is its own colour, and none is the "right" one. Walk round it, and the colours in front pass over the ones
behind and mix, so the globe changes with every step.

## How the public meets it

- **From a distance.** On the canal route, the globe reads first as six coloured circles hanging in the dark, from a
  boat or from the far quay. Water doubles it: every rim is drawn a second time, broken up, on the canal below.
- **Close to.** The globe stands at the waterside on a low plinth, its lowest point about [0.5] m above the ground, so
  people can walk round it and look up through it. The polished edges are lit; the faces are cool and clear.
- **Through it.** Stand on the far side and the city appears through the discs, tinted: a bridge in yellow, a
  facade in violet. Phones will do the rest: the picture everyone takes is the city framed by the world.
- **By day.** The globe is a coloured shadow on the paving. In low winter sun the shadow is long, and walking through
  it puts the colours on your coat.
- **For everyone.** It's silent and steady: no flashing, no strobe, nothing that startles. The light level is
  [to be set with the festival's technicians] for the site, so it sits among the canal's own lights rather than
  over them.

## Materials and making

- **The discs.** Cell-cast acrylic sheet, [25] mm thick, tinted through in six colours, each disc cut flat (in
  segments where the sheet is narrower than the disc) and joined with a clear polymerising cement. The edges are
  diamond-polished, because the rims are what glows.
- **The light.** Low-voltage LEDs, [24] V, sealed (IP67) and fed into each disc's edge inside the base and at the
  joints, warm enough to show each colour truly. The drivers sit in the plinth, away from water, [on a timer set to the

## Two months outdoors

The work is built for an Amsterdam winter, from late November to mid-January, and the plan answers each risk.

| Risk | The answer |
|---|---|
| **Rain, frost, damp** | Acrylic doesn't rust or rot, and takes up only a little water. The LEDs and their connections are sealed to IP67; the drivers are in the plinth, raised off the ground, with drainage |
| **Wind** | The open globe offers far less face to the wind than a solid sphere of its size. The plinth is ballasted to a structural engineer's calculation for the site, and the engineer signs it off [bar lane: stamped sheet] |
| **Cold, damp and movement** | Acrylic moves more than metal: about 0.07 mm per metre for each degree, and a little more as it takes up damp. Every fixing between acrylic and metal floats in a slotted hole with a soft gasket, so the discs can move without stress |
| **Colour over time** | The discs are cut flat, not heat-formed, from colour-stable cast sheet. The maker's colour guarantee covers flat sheet outdoors (it doesn't cover sheet that has been formed), and two months is well within it |
| **Knocks and climbing** | Cast acrylic is far tougher than glass and doesn't break into shards. The globe's lowest point is out of easy reach for climbing, and the plinth has no footholds. A spare disc segment of each colour travels with the work |
| **Cleaning** | Water and a mild soap only, never solvents or glass sprays, which craze acrylic. A cleaning sheet goes to the festival's crew |
| **Nuisance** | None: no sound, no smoke, no lasers, no flashing. The light points inward and along the discs, not out at the windows of the neighbours |
| **Fire** | Acrylic is combustible. The LEDs run cool, the drivers are fused and outside the acrylic, and the electrical work follows the festival's technical rules [bar lane: the electrical note] |

**Installing and removing.** The globe arrives in [N] crated parts: the plinth with its ballast, then the globe,
pre-assembled or in [two] halves. Installation takes [one day with a small crane or a boom lift] and a crew of
[four]; removal is the reverse, and leaves nothing behind.
